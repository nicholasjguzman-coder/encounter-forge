⚔️ Encounter Forge

An AI-agent playtester for Dungeons & Dragons combat encounters · MGT 409 AI Foundations for Managers · Final Project

Encounter Forge lets a Dungeon Master describe a fight in plain English, plays it out 1,000 times in a rules-accurate simulator, reports the odds that the party gets wiped or knocked out, and has an AI agent tune the encounter to the difficulty the DM wants, re-checking every change by simulation.

1. Motivation: who has the problem, and what it costs

Who. Dungeon Masters (DMs), the person at every D&D table who designs and runs the game. Wizards of the Coast reported tracking more than 50 million D&D players worldwide by 2020 [1], and every group needs a DM. I am one: I run a campaign and plan most of its fights myself.

The problem. Before each session, the DM has to decide whether a fight will be fun-hard or campaign-ending. The only official tool is arithmetic: add up the monsters' experience points (XP), apply a multiplier, and compare the total with a per-level threshold (the 2014 Basic Rules) or an XP budget (the 2024 rules). This math ignores what actually decides fights: action economy, area spells, resistances, legendary actions, focus fire and who gets to act first. DMs widely regard it as unreliable, especially at higher levels and with mixed groups of monsters [3][4]. Wizards of the Coast itself said it "changed our methodology" for monster difficulty in the 2024 Monster Manual [4].

Encounter Forge's own simulations show how far apart "the same" official rating can be:

What it costs today.

  • Time. In a poll of 3,663 DMs, 57% said they spend about two hours or more preparing for a roughly four-hour session, and 29% spend three or more [2]. Balancing combat is one of the few parts of that prep that can't be improvised.
  • Frustration and risk. A misjudged fight either wastes an evening (a "speed bump" nobody remembers) or kills characters that players have spent months building. The DM usually finds out only at the table, when it's too late to adjust.
  • Money. Little direct money: D&D is a hobby. The cost is mostly hours and the quality of a shared evening. This project is aimed at a large group of volunteers (DMs), not at saving a firm money.

2. What is new

There are good existing tools. The table below compares them with Encounter Forge feature by feature.

ProductWhat it doesWhat it doesn't do
Kobold Fight Club, D&D Beyond Encounter Builder, AideDD Encounter Builder [5][6] Instant XP-budget difficulty labels. AideDD covers both 2014 and 2024. Formula only, with no simulation: they inherit exactly the problem shown in the table above.
Encounterra (free, Aug 2026) [7] A detailed 5.5e Monte Carlo simulator with maps, line of sight and replays; all SRD classes and monsters. 2024 rules only. Its announcement mentions no plain-English input, no magic-item interpretation, and no automatic tuning toward a target difficulty. Encounters and custom combatants are set up through forms.
DnDCombat, BattleSim, D&D Combat Balancer [8] Combat simulators or probability calculators. Manual setup, no AI agents. BattleSim computes expected values rather than simulating fights.
CALYPSO (LLM DM assistant, research) [9], AI Dungeon / AI DM bots An LLM brainstorms encounters or narrates the game. No measured difficulty: a language model guessing at odds is exactly what a DM can't trust.

What Encounter Forge does differently, and why it matters:

  1. Agents handle the language; code handles the numbers. Five agents turn a DM's messy notes (encounter descriptions, magic items, tactics, homebrew monsters) into validated settings. A deterministic simulator then produces every statistic. No model ever rolls dice or outputs a probability, so the numbers can be trusted.
  2. Closed-loop tuning. Pick "Hard" and the Tuner agent proposes changes that keep the encounter's theme (swap a creature, add a few more, change the distance). Every proposal is re-simulated, and the Tuner sees the measured result before trying again. None of the tools above advertises this.
  3. Answers to the question a DM actually asks. Beyond the TPK chance, the app reports the chance one PC drops, the chance two or more drop, and the share of spell slots, rages, ki and potions the party spends. That last number tells a DM whether the next fight on the same day will be survivable.
  4. Both rule sets, side by side, plus 36 popular non-SRD subclasses, allied creatures, and custom creatures built from a one-sentence description.

3. The agents

How they are organized

The design is a pipeline with one feedback loop. Language agents run first and produce typed, validated JSON. The simulator is the single source of numbers, and the Tuner closes the loop by proposing changes and reading simulated results. Everything between an agent and the simulator passes through code checks (the 🛡️ marks).

Model choice

All agents use gpt-5.6-luna through Portkey. It's available on the course key with no extra setup, it supports JSON-mode output, and it answers in about 1.7–4 seconds per call. Every agent task is translation (text → structured JSON) or short writing, never long reasoning. That's because the hard part, combat math, was deliberately taken out of the model's hands. A larger model would mainly add cost and latency to the parts where accuracy depends on validation and the simulator, not on the model's intelligence. The model name is one constant in dmsim/llm.py (the course page also lists gpt-6-luna, which Portkey accepts too).

A real run, step by step

This is a real fight from my own campaign, re-created in the app. At the table: five level-5 PCs (warlock, bard, fighter, wizard, monk), with an allied veteran, a mastiff and two riding horses (which tried to run away), against an earth elemental and four needle blights. Everyone started in melee range, and no one was surprised. The needle blight isn't in the free SRD, so the run starts by building it.

1Rules Compiler builds the missing creature. I typed one sentence of stats. The agent filled in the rest (ability scores, defenses), said what it assumed, and saved a validated stat block. 1 call · 1,870 in / 760 out tokens · $0.0099.

2Encounter Parser fills the form from plain English. It separated enemies from allies, matched "needle blights" to the new custom creature, and set distance 0 and no surprise. 1 call · 4,822 in / 332 out · $0.0093.

4Simulator plays the fight 1,000 times (about 4 seconds, no model calls), and 5the Explainer writes the briefing. 1,423 in / 397 out · $0.0057.

6Tuner aims for "Hard", the difficulty I originally intended. It made three proposals, each one re-simulated (400 fights per try), and reached the Hard band with four earth elementals. Every version stays in the history and can be reloaded without re-running. 3 calls · 14,428 in (8,744 cached) / 794 out · $0.0161 · about 15 s including simulation.

Whole traced session: 7 model calls, 23,155 input tokens (8,744 cached), 2,765 output tokens, $0.047.

6. Impact

Real user evidence: does the prediction match a real table?

I'm the target user, so the first test was a fight I had already run. My own account of what happened, written before I saw the simulation:

"The players won and none of them (and none of their allies) were downed. That being said, a couple PCs (the monk in particular) were low health at the end of the fight. It was not as hard as I was expecting, both because the elemental wasn't as dangerous as I expected and because I underestimated the value of the allied veteran."

On the decision I actually had to make (how hard is this fight?), the simulation got it right and the official rules got it wrong. It also got the two reasons right. It did not reproduce how hurt the PCs ended up, mainly because simulated casters freely drop Fireball and Thunderwave in melee range without hitting their allies.

Measured numbers

  • Turnaround: one playtest of 1,000 fights takes about 10–12 seconds end to end (about 4 s simulation + about 6–7 s agents). A tuning loop of up to four proposals, each checked by 400 simulated fights, takes 15–20 seconds. Today a DM gets this evidence only by running the fight for real, once, at the table.
  • Quality: the benchmark table in Section 1 shows the official label hiding a roughly 130× difference in TPK risk inside "Deadly". The app shows the actual spread for any encounter, in both editions.
  • Reach and cost: about $0.54 per DM per month in tokens, or $0 in AI-off mode. That's cheap enough to offer free to DMs, a volunteer group of tens of millions of hobbyists [1].
  • Prep time: 57% of DMs spend two hours or more per session [2]. This tool doesn't remove prep, but it replaces the part that is pure guesswork with a 10-second check. A controlled before/after time study is a next step, not something I've measured.
